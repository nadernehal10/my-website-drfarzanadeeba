IVF vs IUI — What Is the Difference and Which One Is Right for You?

IUI and IVF are both effective fertility treatments, but they work very differently and suit different situations. This guide explains both procedures clearly so you can have an informed conversation with your specialist.

Dr. Farzana Deeba August 2026 8 min read

The key difference in one sentence

IUI (Intrauterine Insemination) places prepared sperm directly into the uterus to assist natural fertilisation. IVF (In Vitro Fertilisation) retrieves eggs from the ovaries, fertilises them with sperm in a laboratory, and transfers the resulting embryo back into the uterus.

IUI is less invasive, less expensive, and done first in most cases. IVF is more complex, more effective, and recommended when simpler treatments have not worked or when the cause of infertility makes natural fertilisation unlikely.

How IUI works — step by step

  1. Ovarian stimulation (optional) — oral medication or injections to encourage egg development
  2. Monitoring — ultrasound scans to track follicle growth
  3. Sperm preparation — the partner's sample is processed to concentrate motile sperm
  4. Insemination — a thin catheter places sperm directly into the uterus around ovulation
  5. Pregnancy test — taken 14–15 days later

The procedure takes a few minutes, is generally painless, and most women resume normal activities the same day.

How IVF works — step by step

  1. Hormonal stimulation — daily injections over 10–14 days to stimulate multiple egg production
  2. Monitoring — frequent blood tests and ultrasounds during stimulation
  3. Egg retrieval — a minor surgical procedure under sedation to collect eggs from the ovaries
  4. Fertilisation — eggs are combined with sperm in a laboratory; fertilised eggs develop into embryos over 3–5 days
  5. Embryo transfer — one or two embryos are placed into the uterus
  6. Pregnancy test — taken 14 days after transfer

ICSI (Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection) is a variation of IVF where a single sperm is injected directly into an egg. It is used when sperm quality is poor. In Bangladesh, ICSI is commonly offered alongside IVF for couples with male factor infertility.

When is IUI recommended?

  • Unexplained infertility — no clear cause identified
  • Mild male factor infertility — low-moderate sperm count or motility
  • PCOS with ovulation problems
  • Cervical factor infertility
  • As a first-line treatment before considering IVF

Most specialists recommend 3–6 IUI cycles before moving to IVF, though this depends on age and individual circumstances.

When is IVF recommended?

  • Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes
  • Severe male factor infertility — very low sperm count or motility
  • Failed IUI cycles (typically 3–6 attempts)
  • Advanced age (38 and above) — where time is a significant factor
  • Severe endometriosis affecting the tubes or ovaries
  • Genetic conditions requiring preimplantation genetic testing (PGT)

Which should you try first?

In most cases, IUI is tried first because it is simpler, less expensive, and less physically demanding. However, if investigation reveals a condition that makes IUI unlikely to succeed — such as blocked tubes or very poor sperm quality — IVF may be recommended from the outset.

Age is also a critical factor. For women over 38, the window for IUI is narrower, and many specialists will recommend moving to IVF sooner to avoid losing valuable time.
